Passwords in the Age of AI

One weak point can open the rest. AI makes familiar attacks easier to research, personalize, and repeat. It does not magically break good encryption.

Page 1
Five different openings can expose your accounts.

An attacker only needs one opening: a leaked password, a convincing call, weak recovery, a stolen signed-in session, or a software flaw. Your goal is to keep one opening from spreading everywhere.

1

A password leaks

A password from an old site is tried against email or shopping accounts. AI adds little here; ordinary automation already does most of this.

Best defence: use a generated password for each account and protect email first.

2

The request looks real

AI can imitate writing or a familiar voice and tailor a story using public information. Caller ID, tone, and personal details are not proof.

Best defence: end the incoming contact and call back using a saved number or the number on your card.

3

Recovery is weaker

An old phone number, recovery email, support process, or carrier account can bypass a strong sign-in. AI can help gather details and adapt a script.

Best defence: keep a tested recovery method for your most important accounts.

4

A signed-in device is abused

Malware or a stolen browser session can act as though the device is already signed in. Changing the password may not end that access.

Best defence: update devices, use a strong device passcode, and revoke sessions after suspected compromise.

5

Trusted software has a flaw

AI can help skilled attackers inspect code and adapt known attacks against login, messaging, payment, or password systems.

Best defence: update software you control. If a provider reports a breach, use its official notice, review account changes, and distrust breach-related “support.”

You AI buddy needs limits too

Do not connect a general AI assistant to a password vault or financial account. Avoid broad unattended access to email or a logged-in browser. Never upload password exports, recovery codes, private keys, identity documents, or a complete browser profile. Prefer one task, limited access, and approval before sending, deleting, buying, or changing security settings.

Feasible attack paths, not a claim that every incident uses AI. Updated August 2026

Set up passwords and sign-in

Use a password manager to generate and save a different password for every account. Add stronger sign-in to the accounts that can unlock everything else.

Protect in this order Primary email → Apple account → Password manager → Carrier → Banking

Website passwords

Use your password manager to generate and autofill them. Use at least 20 random characters when the website allows it.

Use a different password for every account. Do not save passwords in email, messages, photos, spreadsheets, or ordinary notes.

The one password you remember

For your password manager, use six randomly chosen words. Do not use a quotation, lyric, personal story, pet, address, or date.

Memorize it and keep one sealed paper copy in a protected physical location.

Phone or computer PIN

Use at least six nonpersonal digits; longer is better. Do not reuse a bank-card PIN or the same PIN across devices.

Face or fingerprint unlock is convenient, but the PIN is still the fallback key.

Use a passkey when offered

For ordinary accounts, save it in the password manager you already use across your devices. Confirm where the browser will save it. Your face, fingerprint, or PIN unlocks the passkey locally; the website does not receive your biometric.

Use a YubiKey for critical accounts

For primary email, Apple, and your password manager where supported, select Security key when asked where to save the passkey. Keep printed recovery codes in a secure physical location.

Buy a pair

Buy two of the same YubiKey.

Buy new from Yubico or an authorized seller.

Use one daily and keep the other as your spare.

1

Open the account’s official Security, Two-step verification, or Passkeys settings.

2

Add the first key, then add the spare separately. If prompted for a location, choose Security key.

3

Sign out and test each key. A spare that was never registered will not get you back in.

4

Carry the everyday key and store the spare elsewhere. Do not label either key with passwords or account details.

Google Authenticator

Turn off cloud sync: tap the cloud icon in the upper-right. The icon should be gray and crossed out.

Lock the app: open Menu → Settings → Privacy Screen.

Keep a way back in: print the recovery codes supplied by important accounts, label them, and store them securely offline. Do not keep the only copy inside the phone, email account, or password manager it is meant to recover.

Recover without creating a new weakness

Prepare while every account still works. During an incident, follow the order instead of improvising from a suspicious message.

Page 3

Primary email

Use a strong daily sign-in, keep printed recovery codes, and remove unknown sessions, forwarding rules, and connected apps.

Apple account

Keep the recovery phone current, review trusted devices, and know which devices and passkeys depend on this account.

Password manager

Use a unique six-word master password and keep sealed offline recovery instructions in a protected location.

One strong sign-in and one backup

Use a strong daily method such as a passkey or YubiKey, plus printed recovery codes or the provider’s recovery process. Remove stale phone numbers, old email addresses, and security questions only after the replacement works.

Before anything goes wrong

Prepare and test

1

Register both YubiKeys separately with each supported critical account.

2

Print fresh recovery codes. Label the service and date; many codes work only once.

3

Separate the backups. Do not keep phone, everyday key, spare key, and recovery paper together.

4

Verify normal sign-in with each key or authenticator. Do not consume the only recovery code merely to test it.

5

Plan for phone loss. Make sure access survives losing both the phone and primary email.

6

Prepare household access. Use provider emergency-access or legacy features, or sealed instructions—not a casually shared master password.

If an account or device may be compromised

Act in this order

1

Stop responding. Do not enter another password, code, or approval prompt.

2

Protect money immediately. Call the bank using the card, statement, or official app—not the message or a search advertisement.

3

Use a different trusted, updated device if the affected device opened a suspicious link or installed software.

4

Secure primary email first. Then replace any exposed or reused password with a generated one.

5

End persistent access. Sign out other sessions; remove unknown devices, passkeys, security keys, and app passwords.

6

Inspect recovery and email. Check phone, email, forwarding, filters, authorized apps, and connected services.

7

Call the carrier if service vanishes unexpectedly or a number transfer is suspected.

8

Preserve evidence. Keep suspicious messages and transaction details, but do not continue the conversation.

Remember: changing a password may not end an existing signed-in session. If untrusted software may have been installed, rebuild the device or obtain qualified in-person help before trusting it again.

Weak pattern: a loop

Digital notes → Password manager → Cloud storage → Digital notes

Each service holds the next secret. Losing one may block recovery; compromising one may spread around the loop.

Stronger patterns: three separate routes

1Remembered passphrase → Password manager → Accounts
2YubiKey pair → Primary email
3Physical safe → Recovery codes

These are three examples. They live in different places and do not all depend on one phone or cloud account.

Look for weak patterns

A route circles back to where it started.
One phone, primary email, or Apple account controls most routes.
Authenticator codes are cloud-synchronized.
Password and second factor share the same locked vault.
The YubiKey pair is kept together or the spare is unregistered.
No route survives losing both phone and email.

Circle a single point of failure. Put an X beside every loop. Star one route to separate or strengthen this week.

Do not write actual passwords, PINs, authentication codes, recovery codes, or private keys on this page. A completed map reveals how your accounts fit together. Store it securely, or destroy it after making the changes you identified.
