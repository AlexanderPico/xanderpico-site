Recursion promised 100 drugs, then proof: what five years of its own words show

Recursion has missed its original plan, produced its first real clinical signals, and not yet shown that either beats existing care. The company’s own words, over five years, are clear enough to say so.

For readers in biotech, AI-native companies and investment research who want the record read plainly. If you have one minute, the box below is the piece.

The verdict, in four lines.
  • Missed. The pre-2025 plan. Of the programs dated in the 2021 annual report, two are stopped or shelved and the third came back as a different study. The 100-program pipeline is now “up to 40” over a decade, and the previous CEO has conceded the timeline.
  • Real. REC-4881 and REC-1245 are the first results in this record I would call genuinely interesting, and the company reports partner milestones worth more than $100 million across nine steps in about two years.
  • Unproven. Neither program has a described comparison group, the company’s own chief medical officer says a later trial must show patient benefit, and most speed claims come with no named benchmark.
  • The tell. Each time a measure was missed or walked back, a new one arrived. The latest, “repeatability,” is the hardest to fail because the company defines it.

What was missed, and how badly?

The March 2021 IPO filing said “Our four lead programs are expected to enroll patients in Phase 2 clinical trials within the next four to five quarters while preclinical programs are progressing.” (S-1). Three programs the next annual report put dates on were REC-2282, REC-4881 and REC-3964. REC-2282 was stopped: the company said the data on it and a second program supported discontinuing the studies outright (Fierce, May 2025). REC-3964 is, in the same article, a candidate for out-licensing. REC-4881 returned years later as a different study.

The bigger miss was scale. In 2024 the CEO said “I think our pipeline will have 100 programs in it in the not-too-distant future.” (GEN). The FY2025 10-K says “Together, we may initiate up to 40 small molecule programs over a decade or longer.” (10-K). In May 2026 Fierce wrote that the promise of 100 medicines in 10 years had not come to pass, and that Gibson, the previous CEO, called it a “maximalist bet” that was wrong “not in direction, but in timeline.” (Fierce).

My judgment: this was not a near miss. The company sold a scale story in 2021 and 2024, and the record shows it did not deliver that scale. The fair counterweight is that early drug programs fail often and the company said so when it stopped them. The unfair reading would be to stop here, because the record after the reset is better.

Why does the standard keep changing?

Read in order, the company’s own sentences move the target. 2021: dated program starts. 2024: a large pipeline. December 2025: “Gone are the days where an interesting model is sufficient. We need models that improve the medicines that we're making.” (GEN). November 2026: “The higher bar we set for ourselves is repeatability. Not one target, not one molecule, or one clinical result, but the ability to do this across programs again and again.” (call).

My judgment: moving from counts to patients is the right direction, and I would not call it spin. But each new standard arrived after the last had been missed or walked back, and “repeatability” is the least falsifiable of the four because it comes with no number. It becomes a test the day the company says how many programs, reaching what stage, by when. Until then, it is a description of ambition.

What is actually good news?

REC-4881 is the best result in the record. The disease, FAP, has no approved therapies, per the 10-Q. On the call, 13 of 15 evaluable patients (about 87%) had a deep reduction in total polyp burden after 12 weeks, and about 85% still had lower polyp burden 12 weeks after stopping. For a condition with no approved drugs, that is a signal worth getting excited about, as long as you keep the next sentence in view. The chief medical officer: “The phase III will determine whether that reduction, that impressive polyp burden reduction that we're seeing, can actually translate into benefit that's meaningful for patients”

REC-1245 is a credible early signal in a hard setting. The company reports three partial responses (two confirmed, in endometrial cancer) and disease control in 11 of 12 patients at 45 mg and above. The 65 mg dose was too high: three of four patients on it had dose-limiting toxicities. Its efficiency claim is the most concrete in the record, and the only one with a named benchmark: “We screened 204 compounds to achieve a development candidate in 18 months, compared to an industry benchmark for small molecules of roughly 2,500 compounds over four years.” If that holds up, it is the strongest argument yet that the platform changes how fast a molecule is found.

The partners are paying. The company says it has “earned more than $100 million across nine milestones tied to the progress in these partnerships” in roughly two years, and more than $535 million in cash inflows from partners in total. Partners committing real money is harder to dismiss than a slide. My judgment: it is the strongest outside evidence in the record. It is evidence that partners like the work and the progress, not that the medicines will work.

What does “AI-discovered” claim here?

REC-4881 and REC-1245 do not mean the same thing. For REC-4881 the company says its model ranked MEK1/2 inhibition highly in APC-deficient cells, and that this insight led it to license the molecule from Takeda. The platform found a use for an existing drug. For REC-1245 the company says “our AI platform guided all three decisions, from biological insight on which target to pursue, how to design the molecule, and which patients to study.” My judgment: credit the platform for the hypothesis in the first case and for the molecule in the second. Calling both “AI-discovered” hides the difference, and the second is the harder claim.

Where does this leave the AI-labs debate?

Analysts asked how Recursion competes as large AI labs move into healthcare. The CEO named three advantages: the data, the closed loop of models and experiments, and having differentiated assets. My judgment: the first two are inputs that outsiders cannot check. The third is the only one the record can test, and it has two entries, both early. The company’s CEO has said both extremes of the AI debate are wrong (“Neither is useful”, Fierce). The Recursion record supports that: no collapse and no revolution, but real early clinical signals, a partnered platform business, and a long history of promising dates it did not keep.

What should the field be asked for?

  • A named benchmark every time “faster” or “fewer” is claimed. Recursion names one of three; I will apply the same test to Relay, Absci and Schrödinger once their November results are in.
  • A date and a number for each standard: how many programs, at what stage, by when.
  • A comparison group before the word “efficacy.”

What I am not saying

  • That the platform does not work. The record shows it produced a hypothesis, a molecule and a patient signal. It does not show those are better than the alternatives.
  • That the results will hold. They are early, in small groups, and the company is the source.
  • Anything about the share price.

What would change my mind

  • Better: the year-end REC-4881 regulatory update arrives with a defined path; the company names the benchmark for its 80%–90% and 30%–60% claims; “repeatability” gets a number and meets it.
  • Worse: the update slips without a stated reason; REC-4881’s registrational start moves beyond the second half of 2027, the plan in the Q3 10-Q; the partnering of REC-617 and REC-3565 is followed by more programs leaving in-house development.
