# Weightlifting, Not Forklifting: Advice for Growing Up with AI

**If you only read this much:** nobody knows what the AI future looks like, so don't bet your kid's childhood on any one version of it. Five rules hold up in all of them.

1. **Let AI carry the heavy loads, not the workouts.** Keep the thinking, writing and struggling that make them stronger.
2. **Hedge.** Teach broad skills, like learning new things and taking initiative, in case the big changes come late, or not at all.
3. **Ask "how do you know?"** of the AI, the internet and you. Curiosity is the best defense.
4. **Protect the human parts:** friends, making things, the outdoors, and what they tell an app.
5. **Don't panic.** Screens aren't poison, and this isn't only your job to solve.

## "What should kids know about AI?" Nobody agrees, and that's the point.

At a Commonwealth Club event in San Francisco on October 19, someone handed the tech journalist Kevin Roose a card: *"What are three key realizations that teens need to know to deal with AI in and out of the classroom?"* ([event](https://www.commonwealthclub.org/events/2026-10-19/kevin-roose-inside-race-create-artificial-superintelligence), [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p6HtVEw6h5M)) It is a question every parent I know is asking in some form.

You can't answer it by guessing the future, because the experts disagree wildly. On that stage Roose put the odds of AI "doom" at 15%, and his interviewer put his own under 1%. The philosopher Nick Bostrom, after two decades on these risks, calls himself "a fretful optimist" with "a degree of moderate fatalism." ([podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=47yk_mg38ls))

So I'd skip the forecast and ask a different question: what helps a kid under every version of the future?

## "Will AI make my kid lazy?" Only if it does the lifting.

Roose's answer is the best I've heard:

> "There are some tasks that are more like weightlifting, and there are some tasks that are more like forklifting. So if you took a forklift to the weight room and had it lift your weights for you, it would do a great job, but you wouldn't get any stronger… And if you did a forklift job with your bare hands, it would make you stronger, but it would be a waste of your human potential."

Notice the second half. The advice isn't "avoid AI." Doing forklift jobs by hand is a waste too. The skill is telling the two apart.

The evidence leans the same way. The OECD, the rich countries' education research body, warns that "when AI removes the productive struggle essential for learning, students may complete tasks faster and achieve better immediate results, but their understanding may be less deeply consolidated." ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/blogs/2026/01/how-to-effectively-use-generative-ai-in-education.html)) In a high-school math study, students with open access to GPT-4 scored about 48% higher on practice problems, then about 17% worse than other students once the AI was taken away. A version that gave hints instead of answers largely avoided the harm. ([Bastani et al.](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122))

AI isn't the villain. Harvard physics students who used an AI tutor built for teaching learned more, in less time, than students in an active-learning class. ([Kestin et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6)) An AI that makes you do the lifting is a spotter. An AI that does it for you is a forklift.

## "Is it still worth learning things, if machines will do everything?" Yes. Hedge.

Elon Musk has said of skills: "Will these skills be necessary in the future? Probably not, because we're gonna be in like a post-work society." ([as reported by Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/news/topics/26/02/50362019/elon-musk-says-you-dont-have-to-go-to-college-because-supersonic-tsunami-of-ai-will-make-learning-skills-unnecessary)) Bostrom was asked what his 16-year-old daughter should study, and gave a more modest answer:

> "It probably makes sense to hedge one's bets at least. Like, cuz what if it doesn't happen? Or what if it takes much longer? And you don't want to end up then kind of entering into the labor force with no skills and no even ability to sort of manage your own life because you've just kind of drifted through your childhood."

Notice which way the hedge points. He isn't saying "AI will take every job, so prepare." He is saying it might not, and a kid who bet everything on it would be stranded. His suggestion is "more general purpose skills like the ability to learn new tools, adapt to new circumstances, to be able to take your own initiatives." That pays off whichever way it goes. If the dramatic future comes late or never, your kid is ready. If it comes fast, they still have judgment, initiative and other people.

## "Can they trust what it tells them?" Not by default. Teach them to ask why.

Musk often says building AI is like raising a child. At the World Governments Summit he said it matters "what sort of values do you instill in that child… to be maximally truth seeking… and I think also curiosity is important." ([summit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=34jrkiYRzMY)) He means machines. I'd flip it: those are the values to raise real children with, and they matter more now that kids have something that answers almost anything instantly, and is sometimes confidently wrong.

In practice that is one habit: **"How do you know?"** - Ask it of the AI, the internet and you. "I'm not sure yet" is a fine answer. - Check a second source. Stanford researchers found that professional fact-checkers leave a website quickly to see what others say about it, while students and even PhD historians tended to judge a site by its own look and claims. ([Wineburg & McGrew](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102)) - Watch for over-trust. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, more confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking. ([Lee, Sarkar et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713778))

Musk's own advice to young people is the same habit at work: "in order to know what to ask the robots for, you need to be able to formulate the question." ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/28/elon-musk-money-wont-matter-gen-z-education-advice-study-arts-sciences-engineering-promot-humanoid-robots-billioniare-tesla-spacex-ceo/))

## "What about AI friends, and what apps do with their data?" Protect the human parts.

*This section is my own conviction. The people I've quoted don't argue it, but the research supports it.*

- **Friends.** A national survey found that 72% of US teens aged 13-17 have used an AI companion, and about one in three found those conversations as satisfying as talking with real friends, or more so. Encouragingly, 80% still prioritise real friendships. ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/nearly-3-in-4-teens-have-used-ai-companions-new-national-survey-finds)) An AI can be good company. It isn't a friend. Roose uses a chatbot for parenting ideas, but draws the line at AI-generated bedtime stories: "That would be too dystopian even for me."
- **Making things and the outdoors.** Roose: "I think the only skill I'm pretty sure will persist is the ability to make things that other people enjoy." That is a prediction, not a finding. But it fits the research on bodies: the WHO says that 80% of adolescents worldwide don't get enough physical activity, which "promotes bone health, encourages healthy growth and development of muscle, and improves motor and cognitive development." ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity))
- **Attention and data.** Apps are often built to keep you scrolling or to learn about you. Bostrom wants young people to "have some ability to control your own information environment rather than just allowing yourself passively to drift hither and thither depending on whatever your social media feed happens to provide to you." Think before sharing personal things with any app. The FTC has asked seven chatbot companies how they "monetize user engagement" and "use or share personal information obtained through users' conversations with the chatbots." ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions))

## What you can do this week

- Ask your kid to sort their schoolwork into two lists: **weights** (thinking, writing, the hard problem) and **forklift** (formatting, busywork, looking up a date). Use AI freely on the second list and carefully on the first. That's Roose's idea.
- Ask "How do you know?" at dinner, about something you read, and about something the AI said.
- Name one thing they could make for someone, and one friend to see in person.
- Look at what an app asks for before they sign up. If you wouldn't tell a stranger, don't tell the app.

If you're the teenager: the same list works for you. Make your own two lists.

## What I'm not saying

I'm not saying screens are poison. The psychologist Candice Odgers warns that the popular claim that screens are "rewiring" children's brains and causing a mental-illness epidemic "is not supported by science." ([Odgers, *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2)) The real world holds things no screen offers, and childhood is when you learn to love them.

I'm also not saying it's only the kids' job. Telling a 13-year-old to out-discipline a system built to hold her attention is like telling her to out-swim a current. Adults and the companies building these tools set the defaults.

We don't know which future our kids will grow up in, and neither does anyone else. We do know what builds a person who can handle any of them: effort on the things that matter, judgment about what to hand off, curiosity, care for other people, and a life lived partly away from the screen.
