ESSAY · THE WAVE AND THE LEASH · November 16, 2026

# The wave and the leash

*People have always handed off aspects of their lives to things bigger and faster than any one of us: muscles to machines, memory to books, coordination to companies. AI is the next handoff, and the first to potentially reach the whole loop of sensing, remembering, learning, deciding and acting.*

## If you read only this

- Every era hands some human capacity to a tool or an institution. Each handoff bought leverage and cost us some practice.
- Many handoffs were not chosen outright; they got harder to refuse as others adopted them. The exits (votes, switching, alternatives) remain, but they cost more as a handoff spreads.
- What sets AI apart is its potential reach. One kind of system could sense, remember, learn, decide and act, and improve its own work, at machine speed.
- Most people will not refuse. They will ride the wave despite fearing the wipeout. The practical work is to decide how much failure we can live with, count it honestly, and have someone independent do the counting, ideally someone whose money or name is on the line.

## Haven't we done this before?

Yes, many times.

| What we lent | To what | What we got | What changed in us (my reading) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Muscle and making | Machines, factories | Goods at a scale no craft could reach | Work set our hours and became an answer to the question of who you are |
| Memory | Writing, books | Knowledge that outlives its holder | We carry less in our heads and more on our shelves |
| Coordination and doing | Companies, governments | Projects no person could run | Much of life is shaped by decisions no one person made |
| Allocation | Markets | Prices that carry knowledge nobody holds | Outcomes without a visible chooser |

Kevin Kelly, in *What Technology Wants*, describes the pattern: we walk less, dig less, calculate less, and are "offloading our remembering to Google." He goes further: "In the long term, the erosion of the traditional self may prove to be a larger part of the technium's cost than its erosion of the biosphere." Institutions fit the same pattern: the science fiction writer Charles Stross calls corporations "very old, very slow AIs."

My reading: each handoff looked like a small trade at the time, a little leverage in and a little practice out. Added up, the trades changed how we live and who we take ourselves to be, mostly without anyone deciding that they should.

## Why do we keep handing things over?

Because it works, and because saying no gets more expensive as others say yes.

Kelly's example is the car. Its success shifts money away from public transport, which makes the bus less attractive and the car more necessary. "The more people who participate," he writes, "the more essential it becomes." Or take work email: once your colleagues use it, opting out means missing the conversation.

The exits are real, though. We vote to change the rules, switch jobs, move, found new communities, and now and then overthrow an arrangement. Plenty of handoffs we welcomed outright. The accurate picture is a gradient: the price of refusal rises as adoption spreads, and different people can afford different prices.

## So what is different about AI?

My reading: its potential reach, and its speed.

A capable system runs a familiar loop: it senses, remembers, learns, decides and acts. Earlier handoffs took over one or more steps in limited contexts. A factory *acts* via production, a ledger remembers, a judicial system decides. Companies and governments run the whole loop, but with people in every seat and at human speed or slower.

Today's AI systems cover some of these steps well and others barely; most do not yet learn from experience as they go. But the trajectory and goal are clear. A system that learned from its own work could get better at it, run its loop in seconds, and be copied.

## Who is still involved?

People, at nearly every step. They build the power plants and chips, choose what a system is trained to do and how it is tested, turn it into products, and decide when and where to use it. That matters because people are where refusal, correction and judgment have always lived.

What may change is how many of them can matter. A company or a state once needed thousands of people, any of whom could stall, leak or quit. A system that depends on a few chokepoints (chips, power, a handful of training teams) has fewer places where refusal bites.

## Will people slow down?

A few may. Kelly shows what deliberate slowness looks like among the Old Order Amish. Their default is "not yet." In Kelly's typical case, an early adopter asks the bishop to try something new, and the bishop agrees on one condition: "you have to be ready to give it up if we decide it is not helping you or is hurting others." The neighbors watch. That is a real path for the few who want it.

Most people will not. They enjoy a good wave as long as they can judge the risk of being wrecked by one. Being wary of a monster wave is not the same as refusing to surf.

What do surfers do? They learn on small waves, wear a leash, read the forecast, surf where there are flags and lifeguards, and stay out when the swell is beyond them. For AI, that comes to three rules:

- **Keep most mistakes small and fixable.** Put the firm limits on what cannot be undone.
- **Decide in advance how much failure you can live with, and count it.** Zero is not on offer, and a system that never risks a mistake rarely does much. Writing about how much rule-breaking a society should tolerate, the security writer Bruce Schneier puts the goal as "a manageable level, but not too low a level." For one AI agent it might be a number you write down and then track, such as no more than one decision in ten reversed in a month.
- **Have someone independent do the counting.** A system that keeps its own score is incapable of winning trust.

## Who can do the counting?

Other institutions with different interests, as we do with companies: courts, auditors, rival firms, regulators. For AI, three checks are taking shape: AI systems that check other AI systems, something like courts; standards that spell out the tests; and insurers who pay when things go wrong. The sections ahead look at all three, starting with the oldest lesson about any checker.

## What does a 1924 park bill teach?

That oversight can be written away in the fine print.

As Robert Caro tells it in 1974 *New Yorker* excerpts from *The Power Broker*, Robert Moses, who built much of modern New York, wrote the 1924 bill that created a state parks council. On its face, the bill said the council answered to the conservation commissioner. Further down it said: "The budget shall be prepared by the State Council of Parks." By precedent, "prepared" meant that the council's chairman, not the commissioner, screened the requests from the regional park commissions and decided which went into the budget. The commissioner saw them only afterward.

Years later, Moses's Triborough authority was due to end once its bonds were paid off. It was written so that "every time it had enough money to pay them in full, it could instead use the money to issue new bonds in their place." The authority never had to end.

Nothing in this story required Moses to break a rule. He wrote them. Control moved through three ordinary-looking places: who wrote the definitions, who controlled the money, and when the authority was due to end.

The same three places decide whether a check on AI works. An AI auditor that tests only what the audited company hands it is a commissioner who sees the budget after someone else has prepared it. An agent that writes the log of its own work also writes the definition of "done." A permission with no end date is a bond that is never paid off.

## Can AI check AI?

Perhaps. The results so far are early and narrow.

In 2018, Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano and Dario Amodei proposed a game they called debate: two AI agents take turns making short statements about a question or a proposed action, and a human judges which agent gave the most true, useful information. The point is to let a person rule on things too complicated to judge directly. They reported results only on a small image-recognition experiment.

In 2023, Ryan Greenblatt and colleagues tested protocols that assume the model may be trying to subvert the checks. In one, a powerful but untrusted model writes code, and a weaker, trusted model rates how suspicious it looks and, if the score is high, edits out the parts that seem to be a problem. In another, a second copy of the powerful model is asked whether the code has a hidden flaw, with measures to keep the two copies from colluding. "These protocols improve substantially on simple baselines," they report, on programming problems.

Both meet Moses's lesson in machine form. A reviewer that shares the author's interests, such as a copy of the same model, can collude. My reading: a reviewer that is independent but weaker can be fooled. And neither result is evidence that courts of AIs will hold up in the world.

## Can insurers and standards keep the score?

Someone independent, ideally someone who pays when the count is wrong. There is precedent. When electricity spread, fire insurers helped fund a small testing laboratory in 1894 that grew into Underwriters Laboratories, according to UL's own history. Rune Kvist, who runs the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company (AIUC), says on the Latent Space podcast that in cases like this "the market runs ahead of regulation to create confidence infrastructure": standards that spell out the tests, and insurers who pick up the bill and so have reason to measure the risk truthfully.

AIUC applies the model to AI agents. Its standard, AIUC-1, requires an independent third party to test the system, a named person accountable when it fails, and updates every quarter. The AI voice company ElevenLabs has bought what Kvist describes as a first-of-its-kind AI agent insurance policy, with Lloyd's of London looking at the data. He argues there is "one job that the labs can never do for themselves, which is to be their own watchdog."

Weigh this as advocacy: Kvist sells it for a living. The episode's show notes name the weak point, which they call the Big Short problem: competing watchdogs can race each other to the bottom. That is Moses's lesson again. A checker is only as good as its independence.

## Will we forget how to do these things?

Not entirely. Kelly tested the idea that old technologies die out. He took one page of farm implements from an 1894-95 Montgomery Ward catalog and found every item, newly made, for sale online. "Nothing was dead," he writes. By his figure, 5,000 amateurs in the United States were knapping fresh arrowheads. My reading: the skills, too, tend to survive in some hands, though mostly as a craft or a hobby rather than as an industry.

So I would not ask whether anyone can still do the job. I would ask whether someone independent can still check the work, using something the system does not control. Not everyone has to be able to do the work. Someone with no stake in the answer has to be able to check it.

## Multiple choice is not authorship

Kelly adopts a rule from the cybernetician Heinz von Foerster: "Always act to increase the number of choices." By that count AI wins easily: more routes, more recipes, more candidates.

But a multiple-choice test shows the gap. You get a list of options, and someone else wrote the question, wrote the options and knows the answer key. A map app offers three routes and you tap the first. A feed holds a million videos and you watch what it puts at the top. In each case the choosing moved to whoever set the defaults.

Authorship is something else. A decision is yours if you can say why you made it, if you could have chosen otherwise at a cost you would accept, and if you would still endorse it after seeing how the choices were set up. Defaults are not bad; good defaults are the point of delegation. The question is whether you can see them and change them.

A technology can add options and move the choosing at the same time. A simple count sees only the first.

## What I am not saying

- That AI is harmless because it is part of an old pattern. Earlier handoffs included long stretches of harm before the controls arrived.
- That handing over is always a mistake. Companies, markets and machines have produced goods no individual could.
- That an AI takeover is coming. I am saying the useful question is who holds the controls and whether they work.
- That today's large language models are the final handoff we will have to consider. Many researchers hold that learning from experience as you go, proper world models and other features of animal and human minds have not yet been realized in today's frontier AI. Another handoff may follow.

## Five questions for any handoff

1. Which part of the loop does it carry: sensing, remembering, learning, deciding, acting?
2. If it is wrong, how wrong, and can that be undone?
3. Who writes its definitions, controls its money and records, and sets when its authority ends? Do they answer to anyone it affects?
4. Did it add options, or only move who chooses?
5. Who counts its failures, using something it does not control, and what do they lose if they count wrong?

The evergreen lesson is that control is rarely taken by force. It is traded in the fine print. Living well with AI means reading the fine print, counting the failures honestly, and keeping hold of the leash.
