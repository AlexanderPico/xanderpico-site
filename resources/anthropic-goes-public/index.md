ARTICLE · FRONTIER LABS AS NATIONAL LABORATORIES · November 19, 2026

# Anthropic goes public... to stay private.

The filing shows who holds the votes, who holds the contracts and where the government's leverage sits. And the needle shifts away from national laboratory status.

## The short version

- **My judgment:** Listing makes the national-lab comparison less convincing. Control sits with the founders and a private trust. Government agencies are under 2% of revenue. The largest dependence is on cloud and chip companies.1
- **What the filing does show:** a government relationship made of one-off levers. A procurement ban, export limits on two models and a threatened emergency-powers order are each disclosed as a business risk.1
- **Where that lands in the tracker:** it supports the “ad hoc levers” path. It does not support any path built on a government-run contract.
- **How sure I am:** fairly sure of what the filing says, moderately sure of what it means, not sure of what happens after listing. This rests on the final prospectus, dated November 18, and on the court record through that day.

## What was sold, and when?

Anthropic priced its offering on November 18 at $83.00 a share. Its Class A common stock began trading on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT on November 19.2,3 The company sold 956,351,354 shares and selling stockholders 23,648,646.1

Separately, seven suppliers and partners (Google, NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC Partners) agreed to buy $26.0 billion of non-voting Class N stock right after the offering.1 Net proceeds to the company from both sales are about $104.8 billion, for “general corporate purposes, including working capital, operating expenses, and capital expenditures, including investments in compute and infrastructure.“1 No government program is named in the use of proceeds.

## Who controls it after the listing?

Not the buyers, and not the government.

A trust, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, has five trustees and holds a special share class that lets it elect a majority of the board.1 The seven co-founders hold, through one company, a single Class F share worth 50.1% of the votes.1 The filing is blunt about what that means: “holders of our Class A common stock generally will not be able to affect the outcome of any matter submitted to a vote of our stockholders or the election of our directors, regardless of how they vote.“1 Counting that vote together with their own shares, the co-founders hold about 61.1% of total voting power, and about 11.0% without it.1 The founders' extra vote starts to wind down once fewer than three of them remain for 90 days.1

My reading: a national lab is government-owned and its operator can be graded and replaced. Here no government holds a share, names a director or can replace anyone. The trust’s trustees are chosen for expertise that includes national security, but the trust is private.1 By search and by reading the governance sections, I found no government equity, golden share or special government right in the filing.

## What does the filing say about government?

Government agencies were under 2% of revenue in every period shown. The filing adds that the risks “are not limited to revenue generated directly from government agencies.“1 Revenue was $33.2 billion for the nine months to September 30, 2026.1

The risks it lists are concrete:

- **Procurement ban.** The company says the President directed federal agencies to stop using its technology “in response to our publicly stated position that our technology should not be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons.“ The Secretary of War also directed that it be designated a “supply-chain risk to national security.“1
- **Emergency powers.** In early 2026 the Department “suggested it could invoke the Defense Production Act to compel us to remove the limitations we have placed on the use of our models for autonomous weapons and mass surveillance.“1
- **Export limits.** In June 2026 Commerce imposed worldwide restrictions on the Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models. The company disabled both for all customers until the restrictions were lifted. It warns that “Similar export controls could recur, could remain in place longer, or could extend to additional models or capabilities.“1
- **Voluntary rules.** The June executive order is “voluntary and does not establish a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement.“1 Anthropic has also agreed with a U.S. evaluation body, which the filing calls the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), to provide model access “for testing and evaluation before and after their public release.“1

None of this changed at pricing. I compared the final prospectus with the November 10 amendment sentence by sentence. In the passages on government and on control, the differences I found are share counts, percentages and one new line: the trust intends to fill a vacant board seat.1,4

## Where does the court case stand?

In the filing’s account (the company’s own description), a federal district court largely granted Anthropic summary judgment on August 27. The D.C. Circuit then denied its petition 2–1 on September 25 and upheld the designation.1 The panel wrote that the Department acted “after Anthropic refused to relax contractual prohibitions on the use of Claude for lethal autonomous warfare or domestic surveillance.“5 Anthropic asked the full court to rehear the case on October 13.6 The government answered on November 18, the day the shares were priced. It called the panel’s decision “case-specific and demonstrably correct” and asked the court to deny rehearing.7 As of November 19 the docket shows no ruling.6 The company says “We cannot predict the ultimate outcome.“1

My judgment: this is the center of the question. The court’s reasoning was that Anthropic’s limits on use were the risk: “the Department reasonably feared that Anthropic might manipulate Claude’s design to prevent it from performing national-security functions that the Department deems contractually authorized and necessary.“5 In my reading, a national-lab operator accepts the owner’s say on use. The founders' letter points the other way: the company will stand up for its values “even when this is costly for us commercially.“8 Listing locks that stance into the governance above. That is a statement by the company, not a result.

## Who does it depend on?

On suppliers, not on government. The company reports about $900.9 billion in purchase and lease commitments, “substantially all” for compute and infrastructure. About $451.6 billion falls before December 31, 2030, and about $70.7 billion within a year of September 30.1 It relies on Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Broadcom. The filing calls the first three partners “which simultaneously serve as our investors, customers, distribution partners, and competitors.“1

Customers are spread out. No single customer exceeded 10% of revenue, and the top five were about 25%. But sales through cloud marketplaces were about 44% of revenue in the nine months.1

## What does the filing leave out?

I searched the full text for “Genesis” and “Department of Energy” and found no matches.1 The national laboratories appear once, in a trustee’s biography.1 The filing does describe a subsidiary that must keep a facility security clearance for classified contracts, and a government product on a FedRAMP High cloud.1 So there is no sign in this filing of government-owned compute, a lab partnership or a government stake.

## How does this map onto the tracker?

Against the nine paths in the paths tracker, as of the [November 19 snapshot](https://xanderpico.com/resources/frontier-labs-paths/snapshots/2026-11-19.html):

- **Supports.** Ad hoc levers: the rehearing request is pending with the government’s reply now filed, the filing warns that similar export limits could recur, and the pre-release access agreement fits that path’s wording. The court signpost moves to “emerging” in the November 19 snapshot, and the estimate goes from 0.90 to 0.92. Company-owned compute, the signpost that counts against a government-owned AI lab: about $900.9 billion of commitments, with proceeds aimed at compute.1,6,7
- **Weakens.** In my reading, any path that needs government to own or run the asset: the government-owned facility, and the equity side of the national-champion bargain. The control structure above leaves no seat for it.1 The snapshot adds a signpost for a listed developer disclosing a government right or binding condition. Status: not yet.
- **Unchanged.** Labs as users of national facilities, the regulated circuit and the international lab. The filing is silent on Energy Department compute and calls the executive order voluntary. It describes Anthropic giving two evaluation bodies access, not those bodies sharing results. The 5% public-benefit fund the founders describe is owned by the company, so it is not a government stake.1,8

## What I am not saying

Not that Anthropic cannot become a national-lab-like partner. Not a view on the shares. Not that the company’s account of its court wins is the court’s.

## What to watch after listing

- **The full-court vote.** Whether the D.C. Circuit hears the case again, now that the government has replied.6,7
- **The first quarterly report.** Whether government revenue stays under 2%.
- **Another export restriction.** The filing says one could recur, so a new one would be a test.1
- **A named federal counterparty.** Any filing naming an agency, a compute-access deal, or a government stake with terms.
