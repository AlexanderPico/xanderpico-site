Four AI-first drug companies, one quarter: everyone is narrowing, almost no one names a benchmark

In one reporting season, four AI-first drug companies told the same story: go it alone less, partner more. They differ on dates, and none names the yardstick behind its speed claims, except Recursion, once.

For readers in biotech, AI-native companies and investment research. The box is the piece if you have one minute.

The verdict, in four lines.
  • Narrowing is the industry, not one company. All four have moved at least one program toward a partner or a spin-out this year or last. Recursion’s turn is not unusual in direction.
  • Dates separate them. Relay’s near-term dates held. Absci kept its data date and moved a trial start about six months. Schrödinger gives financial ranges, not clinical dates. Recursion’s firm dates sit in the second half of 2027.
  • Benchmarks are the gap. Of the speed and efficiency claims in these Q3 documents, one names its comparison. The rest say “faster” or “80%” and stop.
  • What is Recursion’s alone: a new yardstick arriving after each missed one, and two programs flipping from “on plan” to “seek a partner” inside nine months. Relay and Absci show no such yardstick shift.

Is everyone narrowing?

Yes. Schrödinger: “we do not intend to initiate additional clinical trials or advance our other proprietary preclinical programs into clinical trials independently” and “We plan to explore strategic partnerships for the SGR-1505 and SGR-3515 programs to advance the development of these programs beyond our ongoing Phase 1 clinical trials.” (10-Q). In September it put two early programs into a new company, Tectora, for equity, milestones and royalties. Absci: “notwithstanding positive interim Phase 1 results as announced in November 2025, we made the strategic decision to seek a partner for ABS-101 rather than advance it through later-stage development ourselves.” (10-Q). Relay’s first approved medicine, lirafugratinib, was licensed to Elevar in December 2024; the FDA approved it this quarter and the company calls it “the first approved medicine to emerge from Relay Therapeutics” (release). Recursion: “Recursion intends to pursue partnership opportunities to advance REC-617 (CDK7) and REC-3565 (MALT1).” (10-Q).

My judgment: this is a shift in how the field funds clinical risk, not a Recursion misstep. A company with a platform and a few hundred million dollars cannot run many Phase 2 and 3 trials alone, and all four have said so with their feet. The fair question is what the partner structure does to the “AI-discovered medicines” claim: a medicine developed, approved and sold by someone else is evidence about the molecule, and about the deal.

Who keeps their dates?

Relay mostly does. In June it said it would guide enrollment timing later this year. It now says the second-line Phase 3 will “complete enrollment in mid-2027 based on current enrollment trajectory, approximately one year faster than recent benchmarks”, and it has put the regulatory update on its vascular-anomalies program on the calendar: “Relay Therapeutics plans to host a corporate conference call on December 14 after the ASH presentation to review the presented data, provide a regulatory update, and provide an overview of next steps”. One date loosened: triplet data went from the first half of 2027 to “Data update for Phase 1/2 ReDiscover trial triplet combination in 2027”.

Absci kept the data and moved the trial. The 13-week hair-loss readout, once “later this year”, is now December 2026. But the endometriosis study that was “the endometriosis study on this program will start at the end of the year” in June is now “Absci anticipates initiation of this Phase 2 clinical trial in mid-2027.” (release). The release gives no reason. The CEO also calls the 13-week data “directional in nature”, and says Phase 3 doses will rest on the 26-week cohorts.

Schrödinger raised money guidance and dropped a target from view. Full-year annual contract value guidance rose to $222–232 million and drug-discovery revenue guidance to $75–85 million, “due to the recognition of a milestone payment from Structure.” The goal of 75% hosted software by end of 2028 did not come up in the call, release or 10-Q (a phrase search, not a retraction).

My judgment: Relay is the best of the four at being checkable; Absci is the clearest example that a company can hit the headline date and quietly move the one behind it. A date is only worth something if its slips get explained, and Absci’s did not.

Who names a benchmark?

Almost no one. Relay’s “recent benchmarks” are unnamed. Schrödinger says its new AI assistant “now handles approximately 80% of our high-throughput calculations on our internal compute cluster” and that its toxicity tool covers “more than 80 potential off-target proteins”: volumes on its own cluster, with no measure of accuracy against anything outside. Its CEO says customers now see “a very, very, very high probability that the result will be correct”. Recursion’s “204 compounds” against “roughly 2,500 compounds over four years” (see the Recursion essay) is the one named comparison in the set. Absci’s documents contain none that I found.

My judgment: this is the field’s biggest evidence gap, and it applies to all four. “Faster than the industry” is a claim a reader cannot check unless the industry figure is named, dated and sourced. Recursion earns partial credit for doing it once, and loses it by not doing it for the other two.

The money tells different stories

Recursion moved its runway from early 2028 to 2029 after an offering. Relay’s date stayed at “into 2029” while cash rose from $554.5 million to $848.6 million on about $456 million of net share sales; the filings do not say what absorbs the difference (more trials and a commercial build, presumably). Schrödinger cites “at least the next 24 months” on $373 million. Absci has about $173.7 million, down $27.4 million in the quarter, with runway “into the second half of 2028”.

My judgment: runway dates in this group move with financing and spending, not with revenue. Absci is the thinnest: one lead program and a data readout next month that the whole plan leans on.

Where the Recursion verdict holds, and where it does not

Holds: the unnamed benchmarks, the move to partners, the missed 2021 and 2024 promises (those are Recursion’s own history and not tested here). Does not generalize: the pattern of a new standard arriving after each missed one. At Relay and Absci the measure stays the same from quarter to quarter. Schrödinger shifted its yardstick from clinical pipeline to software ACV and hosted share, which is a milder form of the same behavior.

What I am not saying

  • That any of these companies’ science is weak or strong. This compares what they say with what they said.
  • That partnering is a failure. It may be the sensible way to carry a drug through a Phase 3.
  • That Relay is the safe one or Absci the risky one. That would be a view on the stock, and this page has none.

What would change my mind

  • Better: any of the four names the benchmark behind a speed claim; Absci explains the endometriosis move; Recursion gives “repeatability” a number.
  • Worse: Relay’s December regulatory update arrives without a design or date; Absci’s December data is presented without the comparison it describes as directional; a partner for ABS-101, REC-617 or SGR-1505 does not materialize and the program quietly stops.
