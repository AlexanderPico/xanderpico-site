Cognitive tests at scale, for pennies

The spoken script, line by line. Hosts: Curious (Host A) and Kokoro (Host B). AI-generated voices using OpenMOSS-TTSD; reference voices from Qwen3-TTS VoiceDesign and Kokoro.

Plain text · All episodes

Kokoro (Host B): Have you heard of Jev by TypeSafe? It's an unusual AI model: give it text and a question, get a probability. Welcome to Pico Bytes.

Curious (Host A): By contrast, we all know that ChatGPT can draft wordy explanations. But Jev doesn't work that way. It makes a small, explicit judgment you can measure.

Kokoro (Host B): So are we actually talking about taking cognitive tests--designed for humans--and applying them to a Jev model? Like, can it have a first-impression bias?

Curious (Host A): Well, yes. But not to test for a human mind. It's a stress test for developers before building systems that rely on its scores.

Kokoro (Host B): How exactly did they go about doing this in a rigorous way?

Curious (Host A): They worked with a specified Jev version, wrote predictions first, logged calls, and compared with human norms without feeding human answers to Jev.

Kokoro (Host B): I see the benefit: Jev answers in a fifth of a second. Around two hundred twenty thousand calls cost about three dollars and eighty cents. An exceptionally cheap psychology lab.

Curious (Host A): Cheap enough to repeat, changing one thing at a time. First, they adapted Asch's 1946 trait-order test: six personality traits, reversed to compare impressions.

Kokoro (Host B): Right. The impressions of human participants were most strongly influenced by the first traits in the list. Did Jev follow that pattern?

Curious (Host A): No. For example, when negative traits were listed last, Jev rated the person lower. Later information carried more weight—the reverse direction in that comparison.

Kokoro (Host B): So I could change a score by rearranging the same evidence. Did that survive a second test?

Curious (Host A): A later run saw the same direction across list lengths and job-candidate facts. So, yes, builders should calibrate wording and order when working with decision models.

Kokoro (Host B): Moving on to the conjunction fallacy: two things together seeming likelier than one alone. Did Jev make that mistake?

Curious (Host A): It depended on layout. Rated separately, Jev scored the combination above a part on every tested profile, as humans sometimes do. Side by side, it chose the combination on none.

Kokoro (Host B): Same model, different layout. So, again, a potential source of bias users of Jev should pay attention to.

Curious (Host A): Test the exact wording and layout you plan to use in production. Keep the version, the prompt and the results. Otherwise you might not get what you expected.

Kokoro (Host B): What about other scenarios where Jev responds just like a human?

Curious (Host A): On nearly a thousand word pairs, Jev ranked similarity much like human ratings. One striking example was when Jev gave an oddly low similarity score for alcohol and gin.

Kokoro (Host B): Well gin is not my favorite alcohol either...

Curious (Host A): Sure. But wording and order shifted the responses. Reversing a word pair moved individual answers. For a symmetric question, ask both ways before accepting a score.

Kokoro (Host B): A precise-looking number can still be using the wrong ruler.

Curious (Host A): Even an input labeled “silent” changed an answer when it sat beside active inputs and its direction was rewritten.

Kokoro (Host B): That is not silent. That is a stage whisper.

Curious (Host A): Putting inactive inputs in their own list removed the effect. If a field is off, keep it out of the active question.

Kokoro (Host B): Okay. Suppose I want to use Jev as a score or a gate. What do I do before shipping?

Curious (Host A): Pin the version and exact question. Test order, wording and layout. Compare with human ratings where they exist. Calibrate the scale, and keep inactive inputs separate.

Kokoro (Host B): And don't turn one measured system into a replacement for human responses.

Curious (Host A): Right. Cheap calls make careful tests affordable. They do not make the answers themselves reliable.

Kokoro (Host B): The question is part of the instrument. If you change it, measure again.

Curious (Host A): The article and linked result write-ups in the cognitive-tests thread on xanderpico.com have the details, methods and limits. This is independent work, still in progress.

Kokoro (Host B): Thanks for listening to Pico Bytes.
